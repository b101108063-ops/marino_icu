> Inflammation is not itself considered a disease but a salutary operation… but when it cannot accomplish that salutary purpose… it does mischief.
>
> — John Hunter, MD

Inflammation has two opposing "faces": it normally acts to promote healing from tissue injury or infection, but it can also become a source of tissue injury. This chapter describes three life-threatening conditions where inflammation plays a major role: septic shock, toxic shock, and anaphylactic shock.

## Inflammatory Injury

The inflammatory response is designed to eliminate external threats and to cleanse and heal damaged tissues. However, severe or persistent inflammation can produce widespread tissue injury. Inflammatory tissue injury is especially problematic because it can become self-sustaining.

### Neutrophil Activation

In the initial stage of the inflammatory response, circulating neutrophils experience a precipitous rise in cellular O₂ consumption (up to 20-fold) that lasts for 15–20 minutes. This is known as the respiratory burst. The trigger is activation of NADPH oxidase on the cell membrane, which generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) including superoxide radical, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radical.

### Oxidant Injury

ROS are powerful oxidizing agents that are lethal for invading microbes but are also capable of damaging the tissues of the host. Oxidant tissue injury usually begins to appear when the inflammation is persistent or progressive. Hydrogen peroxide moves freely through tissues and can enter the bloodstream and reach distant organs.

### Endothelial Dysfunction

Inflammation promotes endothelial dysfunction from oxidant stress, leading to increased capillary permeability and edema formation. Inflammation also reduces nitric oxide levels via the reaction:

$$O_2^\bullet + NO^\bullet \rightarrow ONOO^-$$

where $O_2^\bullet$ is the superoxide radical, $NO^\bullet$ is nitric oxide, and $ONOO^-$ is peroxynitrite.

## Septic Shock

Sepsis is responsible for one of every 2 to 3 in-hospital deaths in the United States, and one of every 5 deaths worldwide. The mortality rate varies: for septic shock, the mortality rate is about 30% when present on admission, and about 50% when it develops after admission.

### Definitions

1. **Sepsis:** An infection (suspected or confirmed) that is associated with life-threatening organ dysfunction, resulting from a dysregulated host response to infection.

2. **Septic shock:** A subset of sepsis characterized by the need for vasopressor support and by a plasma lactate >2 mmol/L.

### Pathophysiology

#### Hemodynamic Alterations

1. The principal hemodynamic problem is hypotension from systemic vasodilation, attributed to enhanced production of nitric oxide.

2. Oxidant injury in the vascular endothelium leads to fluid extravasation and hypovolemia.

3. Proinflammatory cytokines promote cardiac dysfunction, but the cardiac output is usually increased as a result of tachycardia and a decrease in left ventricular afterload.

4. Splanchnic blood flow is typically reduced despite increased cardiac output.

Because of the high cardiac output and peripheral vasodilation, septic shock is also known as hyperdynamic shock or warm shock.

#### Mitochondrial Dysfunction

The organ dysfunction in sepsis is attributed to a defect in the production of high-energy ATP molecules in mitochondria. This would explain the observation that tissue PO₂ in skeletal muscle is increased in patients with severe sepsis.

### Implications

1. Since tissue O₂ levels are not impaired in septic shock, the liberal use of supplemental O₂ is not justified.

2. The increase in plasma lactate levels in septic shock is not the result of inadequate tissue oxygenation. Lactate production increases during periods of metabolic stress, where lactate serves as an alternative energy source.

### Monitoring

1. The central venous O₂ saturation ($ScvO_2$) will be inappropriately increased in septic shock because of decreased O₂ extraction.

2. The PCO₂ gap is not influenced by systemic oxygenation and can be useful for identifying tissue hypoperfusion.

3. Plasma lactate levels have prognostic significance, and normalization of lactate levels is an important goal.

### Management

**Table 17.2** The Initial Management of Septic Shock

| Intervention | Recommendations |
|---|---|
| **Volume Infusion** | 30 mL/kg (ideal body weight) of crystalloid fluid, infused within 3 hours of presentation |
| **Vasopressor Therapy** | Can be initiated through a peripheral vein proximal to the antecubital fossa. Begin with norepinephrine, titrate to achieve MAP ≥65 mm Hg. If norepinephrine dose is 0.25–0.5 μg/kg/min, add vasopressin. If necessary, add epinephrine as a 3rd vasopressor |
| **Corticosteroid Therapy** | If norepinephrine dose is >0.25 μg/kg/min, give IV hydrocortisone 50 mg every 6 hours |
| **Antimicrobial Therapy** | Initiate within 1 hour of presentation. Obtain appropriate cultures prior to first dose |

### Vasopressors

**Preferred agents:** Norepinephrine is the initial vasopressor of choice. If hypotension persists despite norepinephrine 0.25–0.5 μg/kg/min, add vasopressin. If refractory, add epinephrine.

### Corticosteroids

Corticosteroids have two actions potentially beneficial in septic shock: anti-inflammatory activity and facilitation of the vasoconstrictor response to catecholamines. Corticosteroid therapy is suggested for patients with septic shock receiving norepinephrine or epinephrine at ≥0.25 μg/kg/min.

### Antimicrobial Therapy

Prompt initiation of antimicrobial therapy is a cornerstone. At least 2 sets of blood cultures are recommended. Empiric coverage should have broad-spectrum activity and coverage for MRSA if indicated.

### Toxic Shock Syndrome

Toxic shock syndrome (TSS) is a specialized form of septic shock caused by toxin-producing strains of Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pyogenes.

**Table 17.3** Comparative Features of the Toxic Shock Syndromes

| Feature | Staphylococcal TSS | Streptococcal TSS |
|---|---|---|
| **Notable Sources** | Menstruation (tampons), Surgical wounds, No source (35%) | Necrotizing fasciitis, Postpartum sepsis, No source (45%) |
| **Positive Blood Cultures** | <5% | 60% |
| **Antibiotic Therapy** | MSSA: cefazolin + clindamycin; MRSA: vancomycin + clindamycin | High-dose penicillin + clindamycin |
| **Mortality Rate** | Menstrual <2%; Nonmenstrual 6% | 35% |

#### Clinical Presentation

TSS typically begins with an influenza-like illness (fever, myalgias, lethargy), often with diarrhea and a diffuse erythematous rash. This progresses within 48 hours to hypotension and multiorgan involvement.

#### Management

1. **Hemodynamic management** similar to septic shock.
2. **Antimicrobial therapy** including clindamycin (which inhibits toxin production).
3. **Source control:** removing tampons/intrauterine devices, draining abscesses, debriding necrotizing infections.

## Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is an acute multiorgan dysfunction syndrome produced by the immunogenic release of inflammatory mediators from basophils and mast cells. Deaths from anaphylaxis are rare, with a mortality rate of 0.25%–0.33%.

### Clinical Features

Anaphylactic reactions typically appear within minutes of exposure and can recur within 72 hours. Edema and swelling in the involved organ is characteristic.

**Table 17.4** Signs and Symptoms of Anaphylaxis

| Symptom | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Urticaria and angioedema | 60–90% |
| Upper airway angioedema | 50–60% |
| Flushing | 45–55% |
| Dyspnea, wheezing | 45–50% |
| Hypotension, syncope | 30–35% |
| Abdominal pain, diarrhea | 25–30% |
| Acute coronary syndrome | 4–5% |

### Treatment

#### Epinephrine

Epinephrine blocks the release of inflammatory mediators and is the only drug capable of stopping an anaphylactic reaction. The usual dose in adults is 0.3–0.5 mg (0.3–0.5 mL of 1:1000 solution) given by deep intramuscular injection in the anterolateral thigh, repeated every 5–15 minutes as necessary.

**Table 17.5** Aqueous Epinephrine Solutions and Their Clinical Uses

| Dilution | Condition | Dosing |
|---|---|---|
| 1:10,000 (0.1 mg/mL) | Asystole or PEA | 10 mL (1 mg) IV every 3–5 min |
| 1:1,000 (1 mg/mL) | Anaphylaxis | 0.3–0.5 mL (mg) IM every 5–15 min |
| 1:100,000 (10 μg/mL) | Anaphylactic shock | Infuse at 5–15 μg/min |

**Glucagon:** For anaphylactic reactions refractory to epinephrine in patients receiving β-blockers, glucagon can be effective. Dose is 1–5 mg IV over 5 minutes, followed by continuous infusion at 5–15 μg/min.

### Second-Line Agents

- **Antihistamines:** Diphenhydramine 25–50 mg PO/IM/IV and ranitidine 50 mg IV or 150 mg PO.
- **Bronchodilators:** Inhaled β₂-agonists like albuterol.
- **Corticosteroids:** Have no role in acute management of anaphylaxis.

### Anaphylactic Shock

Anaphylactic shock is characterized by the same hemodynamic abnormalities as septic shock, but the hypotension can be more pronounced because of exaggerated fluid loss through leaky capillaries.

**Management:**
1. **Epinephrine:** IV epinephrine and aggressive volume resuscitation.
2. **Volume Resuscitation:** As much as 35% of intravascular volume can be lost through leaky capillaries.
3. **Refractory Hypotension:** Give glucagon if the patient has received a β-blocker recently. Otherwise, add norepinephrine or phenylephrine.

## A Final Word

### Inflammation (not hypoxia) as the Major Cause of Death in ICUs

1. Septic shock is the leading cause of in-hospital deaths in the United States.

2. The source of organ failure in septic shock is mitochondrial dysfunction, not inadequate tissue oxygenation, and the source of mitochondrial dysfunction is inflammation.

The damaging effects of inflammation are largely due to oxidation, so promoting tissue oxygenation may have an effect diametrically opposed to what is intended.
