## Chapter 52: Pharmaceutical Drug Overdoses

> "A desire to take medicine is, perhaps, the great feature which distinguishes man from other animals."
> — Sir William Osler

Deaths from drug overdoses have increased more than 5-fold in the past 20 years — from 20,000 deaths in 2000 to 108,000 deaths in 2022. This chapter describes some of the more frequent or problematic pharmaceutical drug overdoses, including those from acetaminophen, benzodiazepines, beta-blockers, opioids, and salicylates.

---

## Acetaminophen

Acetaminophen is a ubiquitous over-the-counter analgesic-antipyretic agent included in over 600 commercial preparations. It is the leading cause of acute liver failure in North America, Europe, and Australia. In the United States, it is responsible for almost half of the cases of acute liver failure, and about half of overdoses are unintentional.

### Toxic Mechanism

The bulk of acetaminophen metabolism involves formation of nontoxic sulfate and glucuronide conjugates. About 10% involves oxidation by cytochrome P450 enzymes to form a toxic metabolite. When daily doses are excessive (>4 g/day), the nontoxic pathway becomes saturated, diverting metabolism to the toxic pathway. The toxic metabolite is normally removed by conjugation with glutathione. When glutathione levels fall by 70%, the toxic metabolite accumulates and causes hepatocellular injury.

### Toxic and Lethal Doses

- **Toxic dose:** 7.5–15 grams daily in most adults (many poison centers use 10 grams as a threshold requiring investigation)
- **Maximum safe adult dose:** 4 grams/day (some guidelines recommend ≤3 grams/day for chronic use)
- **Lethal dose:** >250 mg/kg or >12 g

### Predisposing Conditions

The following conditions increase susceptibility to hepatotoxicity at lower doses:

1. **Malnutrition or prolonged fasting** — depletes hepatic glutathione stores
2. **Chronic alcohol use disorder** — increases cytochrome P450 activity and contributes to glutathione depletion
3. **Chronic liver disease** — considered high risk (though evidence is not convincing)
4. **Drugs that stimulate cytochrome P450** — carbamazepine, phenytoin, INH, rifampin; opioids compete for the nontoxic conjugation pathway

### Clinical Presentation

| Stage | Timing | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Stage 1** | First 24 hours | Absent or nonspecific symptoms (nausea, vomiting, malaise); no evidence of hepatic injury |
| **Stage 2** | 24–72 hours | Rising hepatic aminotransferases (AST, ALT; can reach 10,000 IU/L); jaundice, prolonged INR; possible renal impairment |
| **Stage 3** | 72–96 hours | Peak hepatic injury; hepatic encephalopathy; progressive lactic acidosis; acute oliguric renal failure; multiorgan failure; death |

### Risk Assessment

Plasma acetaminophen levels obtained 4–24 hours after acute ingestion can predict hepatotoxicity risk using the **Rumack-Matthew nomogram**:

- **Above treatment line** (150 mcg/mL at 4 hrs to 5 mcg/mL at 24 hrs): Warrant antidotal therapy with N-acetylcysteine
- **Above high-risk line** (300 mcg/mL at 4 hrs to 10 mcg/mL at 24 hrs): 90% chance of hepatotoxicity; may require extended N-acetylcysteine treatment

> The nomogram is useful only for acute ingestions with accurately known timing. For extended-release preparations, repeat levels 4–6 hours after an initial non-elevated reading.

### N-Acetylcysteine (NAC)

NAC is a cysteine surrogate that is the antidote for acetaminophen hepatotoxicity. Indicated when plasma acetaminophen level is above the treatment line or when hepatic aminotransferases are elevated.

**Intravenous regimen:**

**TABLE 52.1 — N-Acetylcysteine Dosing for Acetaminophen Toxicity**

| Phase | Dose | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| **Loading** | 150 mg/kg IV over 1 hour | — |
| **Second** | 50 mg/kg IV over 4 hours | — |
| **Third** | 100 mg/kg IV over 16 hours | — |
| **Continuation** (if goals not met) | 6.25 mg/kg/hr IV | Until goals achieved |

**NAC stopping criteria:**

1. Serum acetaminophen level is undetectable
2. AST, ALT, and bilirubin are decreasing
3. INR is normalizing
4. Patient is asymptomatic

## Benzodiazepines

Benzodiazepines are second only to opiates as drugs most frequently involved in medication-related deaths. However, they are rarely fatal when ingested alone — other respiratory depressant drugs are almost always involved in fatalities.

### Clinical Features

In pure benzodiazepine overdoses:

- Deep sedation but rarely coma
- Respiratory depression (2–12%), bradycardia (1–2%), hypotension (5–7%) are uncommon
- Agitated confusional state with hallucinations is possible (can mimic alcohol withdrawal)

Note: There are no serum assays for benzodiazepines, and urine screening can miss common agents like lorazepam. Diagnosis is typically based on clinical history.

### Management

General supportive care (blood pressure support, mechanical ventilation as needed).

**Flumazenil** is a benzodiazepine antagonist (binds GABA-A receptors with minimal agonist activity). It reverses benzodiazepine-induced sedation but is inconsistent for respiratory depression.

- **Dosing:** 0.2 mg IV boluses, repeated every few minutes to a cumulative dose of 1 mg. Onset in 1–2 minutes; peak effect at 6–10 minutes; duration ~1 hour.
- **Continuous infusion:** 0.3–0.4 mg/hr to reduce re-sedation risk.
- **Adverse effects:** Nausea, vomiting, agitation, and (rarely) seizures; minor and major adverse events are more frequent than with placebo.

## Beta-Blocker Overdose

Beta-blocker overdose can cause severe cardiovascular toxicity including bradycardia, hypotension, and cardiogenic shock.

### Mechanisms of Toxicity

Beta-blockers antagonize beta-adrenergic receptors, reducing:

- Heart rate and contractility
- AV nodal conduction
- Cardiac output

Severe cases can produce refractory cardiogenic shock.

### Management

**Glucagon** is the first-line treatment:

- **Dosing:** 3–5 mg IV bolus in adults; response should be evident within 3 minutes. Can repeat 5 mg bolus if initial response is unsatisfactory.
- **Chronotropic response** is optimal when plasma ionized calcium is normal.
- **Continuous infusion:** 5 mg/hr after initial bolus response.
- **Adverse effects:** Nausea/vomiting (refractory to ondansetron at >5 mg/hr), hyperglycemia, hypokalemia, catecholamine release causing hypertension in patients with hypertension.

**TABLE 52.2 — Management of Beta-Blocker Toxicity**

| Drug/Agent | Role |
|---|---|
| Glucagon | First-line; 3–5 mg IV bolus; then 5 mg/hr infusion |
| Phosphodiesterase inhibitors (milrinone) | Consider if no response to glucagon; vasodilators may cause hypotension |
| High-dose insulin/euglycemic clamp | Consider in refractory cases |
| Lipid emulsion | Consider for lipophilic beta-blocker overdose |
| Temporary pacemaker | For persistent bradycardia |

## Opioid Overdose

The opioid overdose crisis in the United States involves primarily prescription opioids rather than street drugs.

### Clinical Features

The classic description (stupor, pinpoint pupils, bradypnea) is not reliably present. The response to naloxone is the most reliable method of identifying opioid overdose.

### Naloxone (Narcan®)

Naloxone is a **pure opioid antagonist** that blocks mu (μ) receptors (responsible for analgesia, euphoria, and respiratory depression). It can be given intranasally or intravenously.

**Intranasal naloxone:**

- Available as 2 mg or 4 mg single-dose nasal spray (0.1 mL diluent)
- 2 mg is probably sufficient in most cases
- Average response time: ~3 minutes; peak blood levels at ~20 minutes
- Elimination half-life: 1.5–2 hours
- If no response within 3 minutes, give second dose in the other nostril; for 4 mg, consider dividing into two 2 mg doses (one per nostril)
- Opioid effects return after 1–2 hours; follow with continuous IV infusion at two-thirds of the effective dose (diluted in 250–500 mL saline, infused over 6 hours)

**Intravenous naloxone:**

- For depressed sensorium without respiratory depression: 0.4 mg IV; repeat in 2 minutes if needed
- For respiratory depression: 2 mg IV push; double to 4 mg if no response in 2–3 minutes; further doses up to 15 mg; no response to 15 mg indicates non-opioid cause
- Follow with continuous IV infusion (same dosing as intranasal)

**Adverse reactions:**

- Opioid withdrawal (most common)
- Case reports of acute pulmonary edema with high-dose naloxone

## Salicylate (Aspirin) Poisoning

Salicylate poisoning is second only to acetaminophen as a cause of suicide-related deaths from over-the-counter analgesics. Ingestion of 10–30 grams (150 mg/kg) can be fatal.

### Pathophysiology

Once ingested, acetylsalicylic acid is converted to salicylic acid, the active form.

**Respiratory alkalosis:** Salicylic acid directly stimulates respiratory centers in the lower brainstem, producing increased respiratory rate and tidal volume, causing a decrease in PaCO₂ (an early sign).

**Metabolic acidosis:** Salicylic acid uncouples oxidative phosphorylation, increasing glycolysis and lactate production. The accumulation of lactic acid (and ketoacids) produces a high anion-gap metabolic acidosis. This is a relatively late complication, and an acidemic pH is a poor prognostic sign.

**Other effects:** Increased metabolic rate → hyperthermia, diaphoresis, dehydration.

### Clinical Manifestations

- Early: Hyperpnea, tinnitus, vertigo, nausea, vomiting (within first few hours)
- Later: Agitation, fever
- Severe: Coma, cerebral edema, ARDS, hemodynamic instability
- Labs: Hypocapnia, respiratory alkalosis, lactic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hypernatremia, acute kidney injury

### Diagnosis

**TABLE 52.3 — Salicylate Levels and Clinical Significance**

| Level | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| 10–30 mg/dL | Therapeutic range |
| >40 mg/dL | Toxic |
| >75 mg/dL | Life-threatening |
| >90 mg/dL | Hemodialysis indicated |

*Note: Chronic salicylate toxicity often presents with lower levels.*

### Management

**Activated charcoal:** 1 g/kg (max 100 g) every 4 hours until charcoal appears in stool or levels begin to decrease. Most effective if given within 2 hours of ingestion.

**Urinary alkalinization:** Increases salicylate excretion. Add 40 mEq KCl to 150 mEq/L NaHCO₃ solution. Maintain urine pH >7.5. Continue until salicylate levels fall to therapeutic range.

**Hemodialysis:** Indicated for:

- Plasma salicylate level >90 mg/dL (>6.5 mmol/L)
- Level >80 mg/dL (>5.8 mmol/L) plus impaired renal function
- Life-threatening intoxication (cerebral edema, ARDS, multiorgan failure)
