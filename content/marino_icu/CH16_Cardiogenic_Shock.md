> When is a piece of matter said to be alive? When it goes on "doing something," moving, exchanging material with its environment.
>
> — Erwin Schrödinger

The human organism exchanges material with its environment by virtue of a circulatory system equipped with an automatic stroke pump that averages about 100,000 stroke cycles daily, and pumps a daily volume of about 8,000 liters. Failure of this circulatory system begins and ends with cardiac pump failure, which can be categorized as "concerning" (conventional heart failure), "life-threatening" (cardiogenic shock), or "catastrophic" (cardiac arrest).

This chapter focuses on cardiogenic shock, where management is designed to allow time for the heart to heal from a recent insult, or for a definitive procedure to be performed. The real challenge is that half of the patients do not survive despite the best management efforts.

## Pathophysiology

Cardiogenic shock can be described as cardiac pump failure that results in inadequate O₂ delivery to tissues and impaired oxidative metabolism. The culmination of this condition is multiorgan failure and a fatal outcome.

### Etiologies

Acute myocardial infarction is responsible for about two-thirds of the cases of cardiogenic shock, while the remaining cases arise from a variety of cardiac disorders.

#### Acute Myocardial Infarction

Cardiogenic shock develops in 5–10% of cases of acute myocardial infarction. Infarction of the left ventricle is responsible for about 80% of the cases, and requires loss of at least 40% of the functional mass of the ventricle.

There are three infarct-related complications that can produce cardiogenic shock:

1. **Rupture of the papillary muscles** (7% of cases): results in acute and life-threatening mitral insufficiency.

2. **Rupture of the interventricular septum** (4% of cases): is accompanied by a large left-to-right shunt, which floods the pulmonary circulation.

3. **Rupture of the ventricular wall** (1% of cases): results in a rapidly-accumulating hemopericardium and pericardial tamponade.

#### Other Conditions

**Table 16.1** Causes of Cardiogenic Shock Other than Acute MI

| Category | Conditions |
|---|---|
| **Chronic Conditions** | Chronic heart failure with acute decompensation, Acute myocarditis, Takotsubo's cardiomyopathy, Dynamic outflow obstruction |
| **Acute Circumstances** | Postcardiotomy shock, Peripartum cardiomyopathy, Post-cardiac arrest shock |
| **Valvular Disease** | Advanced valvular disease |

**Dynamic Outflow Obstruction:** In cases of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, the flow of blood through the narrowed left ventricular outflow tract can create a suction effect that pulls the mitral valve leaflets anteriorly, creating a "dynamic left ventricular outflow obstruction." Recognition is important because it is treated differently: the goal is to slow the heart rate using nonvasodilating beta-blockers.

**Cardiac Surgery:** Cardiogenic shock is reported in 2–5% of patients who undergo cardiac surgery. Mechanisms include myocardial hibernation or "stunning" and inadequate cardioprotection.

**Pregnancy:** Peripartum cardiomyopathy can appear in the later stages of pregnancy or early months following delivery. The incidence is 1 case per 3,000–4,000 pregnancies in the United States.

### Obstructive Shock

The category known as "obstructive shock" is essentially cardiogenic shock caused by conditions extrinsic to the heart: acute pulmonary embolism, pericardial tamponade, and tension pneumothorax. The hemodynamic consequences are the same as cardiogenic shock.

### Hemodynamic Changes

The hemodynamic changes in cardiac pump failure include a decrease in cardiac output accompanied by an increase in cardiac filling pressures and an increase in systemic vascular resistance. Cardiogenic shock is distinguished from heart failure by the presence of tissue hypoperfusion and inadequate tissue oxygenation.

**Right Heart Failure:** Cardiogenic shock from right ventricular (RV) failure has a lower mortality rate than left ventricular failure. Features that identify RV failure include an increase in the CVP/PAWP ratio, a dilated RV, and a normal LV ejection fraction.

### Exceptions

The cardiac index in cardiogenic shock is typically <2.2 L/min/m², but cardiac filling pressures are not increased in as many as 30% of patients with infarct-related cardiogenic shock. Hypotension is absent in about 5% of cases.

### The Microcirculation

Studies using sublingual videomicroscopy have shown that cardiogenic shock is associated with a decrease in capillary perfusion that is independent of the changes in global hemodynamic measures, and it has a greater impact on mortality than the macrocirculatory changes.

### Systemic Inflammation

Signs of systemic inflammation have been reported in 20–40% of patients with cardiogenic shock complicating acute myocardial infarction, and these patients have a lower SVR and a higher mortality rate.

## Management

Since most cases of cardiogenic shock are associated with acute myocardial infarction, management often includes measures aimed at coronary revascularization. The focus here is on measures that promote tissue perfusion.

### Monitoring

The management of cardiogenic shock requires a reliable measure of the cardiac output. The most reliable measurement available is the thermodilution cardiac output provided by pulmonary artery (PA) catheters. The use of PA catheters has been shown to improve outcomes in cardiogenic shock.

### Goals of Management

**Table 16.2** Goals of Management in Cardiogenic Shock

| Category | Goals |
|---|---|
| **Hemodynamics** | PAWP = 19–20 mm Hg; CI ≥2.5 L/min/m²; SVRI = 25–30 Wood Units; Mean BP ≥65 mm Hg |
| **Tissue Perfusion** | PCO₂ Gap <6 mm Hg; Urine output >0.5 mL/kg/h |
| **Tissue Oxygenation** | SvO₂ >50%; Plasma lactate <2 mmol/L |

### Vasopressor Therapy

Hypotension is almost universal in cardiogenic shock, and vasoconstrictor agents are used to raise the blood pressure. The vasopressor of choice in cardiogenic shock is norepinephrine.

**Norepinephrine:** The preference comes from studies showing that norepinephrine had a lower risk of tachycardia and troublesome arrhythmias compared to other vasopressors. Dosing: start at 5 μg/min (or 0.05 μg/kg/min) and titrate upward.

### Inotropic Therapy

Vasopressor therapy does not alleviate cardiac pump failure, and drugs that increase cardiac contractile force (inotropic agents) are typically added.

**Table 16.3** Inotropic Agents for Cardiogenic Shock

| Agent | Dosing Regimen |
|---|---|
| **Dobutamine** | Start at 3–5 μg/kg/min, increase in increments of 3–5 μg/kg/min if needed. Usual dose is 3–20 μg/kg/min. Major disadvantage is an increase in heart rate. |
| **Milrinone** | Initial dose is 50 μg/kg (over 10 min), followed by infusion rate of 0.375–0.75 μg/kg/min. Dose adjustments advised for renal insufficiency. Major disadvantage is hypotension. |
| **Levosimendan** | Initial dose is 12 μg/kg (over 10 min), followed by infusion rate of 0.1 μg/kg/min. After 1 hour, infusion rate can be increased to 0.2 μg/kg/min if needed. Has a long half-life (80 hrs). Not approved in the United States. |

### Mechanical Cardiac Support

Mechanical cardiac support (MCS) is generally reserved for cases of refractory cardiogenic shock where improvement in cardiac function is expected, or another intervention is planned.

#### Intra-Aortic Balloon Pump

The intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) is the oldest form of MCS. The intra-aortic balloon is inserted percutaneously into the femoral artery and advanced up the aorta until the tip lies just below the origin of the left subclavian artery.

**Mechanism:**
- Inflation during diastole increases peak diastolic pressure, augmenting coronary blood flow.
- Deflation at the onset of systole creates suction that reduces left ventricular afterload.

**Complications:** Limb ischemia is the principal concern. Other complications include septicemia, balloon rupture, peripheral neuropathy, and pseudoaneurysm.

#### Impella Catheters

Impella catheters provide flow assistance for ventricular output. The catheter is equipped with a miniature centrifugal pump and is inserted percutaneously into the femoral artery and advanced into the left ventricle. The pump moves blood from the left ventricle to the proximal aorta at flow rates of 2.5–5.5 L/min.

**Complications:** Major bleeding and limb ischemia are more common with Impella catheters than with IABP. The incidence of significant hemolysis (5–10%) is also greater.

#### ECMO

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is designed to support both circulatory flow and pulmonary gas exchange. Veno-arterial ECMO (VA-ECMO) is used for cardiogenic shock.

VA-ECMO offers advantages including the ability to provide full biventricular support and the ability to support oxygenation and CO₂ removal. It is the preferred method when cardiogenic shock is accompanied by respiratory failure.

**Complications:** Major bleeding (41%), sepsis (30%), limb ischemia (17%), lower extremity compartment syndrome (10%), stroke (6%), and lower limb amputation (5%).

**LV Afterload:** VA-ECMO increases left ventricular afterload, which can reduce LV output and cause acute pulmonary edema. Efforts to tackle this include combining IABP or Impella catheters with VA-ECMO.

### Outcomes

The largest registry of ECMO patients has reported a 42% survival rate to hospital discharge when VA-ECMO is used for cardiogenic shock.

### Device Selection

VA-ECMO is the method of choice for cardiogenic shock associated with respiratory failure. Otherwise, there is no consistent evidence showing that one method is superior to the others. Mechanical support that is initiated after the onset of multiorgan failure has been described as a "bridge to nowhere."

## A Final Word

1. The management of cardiogenic shock is aimed at "buying time" until the heart begins to heal or another intervention is performed.

2. Pharmacological support is an immediate need, but this approach increases cardiac workload and can be counterproductive.

3. Mechanical support devices can "unload" the ventricle (IABP), provide flow assistance (Impella), or provide support for gas exchange and circulatory flow (VA-ECMO).

4. Avoiding delays to initiation of mechanical support will optimize outcomes.
