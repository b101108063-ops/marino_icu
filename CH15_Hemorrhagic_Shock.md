## Chapter 15: Hemorrhagic Shock

It is a bad sign in acute illnesses when the extremities become cold.

Hippocrates Aphorisms, 400 B.C.

Trauma is the leading cause of death globally in the 25–49 year age group (1), and the most
treatable cause of death in these cases is hemorrhagic shock. The resuscitation of traumatic blood
loss presents many challenges (as described in this chapter), and one of these is the design of the
circulatory system, which operates with a limited volume and a volume-sensitive stroke pump.
This is an energy-efficient design that limits the workload of the heart, but it creates an
intolerance to blood loss; e.g., whereas the lungs, liver, and kidneys can lose as much as 75% of
their functional mass without a fatal outcome, loss of as little as 30% of the (limited) blood
volume can be fatal.

This chapter describes the physiology and classification of acute blood loss, and presents the
relevant features and strategies involved in the resuscitation of hemorrhagic shock, with an
emphasis on life-threatening blood loss from traumatic injuries.

### 導讀摘要

- **失血分類**：Class I（<15%）通常無症狀不需輸液；Class III（31–40%）開始出現去代償性休克；Class IV（>40%）可能不可逆
- **血紅蛋白/血球容積（H&H）不可靠**：急性失血時 H&H 不會立即下降；H&H 反映的是復甦輸液量，而非失血量
- **Damage Control Resuscitation**：1:1:1 比例（FFP:RBC:Platelet）、限制晶體液、允許性低血壓（收縮壓 80–90 mmHg）
- **凝血障礙（TRAUMA-INDUCED COAGULOPATHY）**：40% 戰創傷人在入院時已有 TIC；活化蛋白 C 是主要凶手
- **Thromboelastography（TEG）**：比傳統凝血檢查更敏感，可指導止血治療

### Acute Blood Loss

The normal blood volume in adults is estimated at **66 mL/kg** (lean body weight) for males, and
**60 mL/kg** for females (2). For an adult male with a lean body weight of 75 kg, the estimated
blood volume is **5 liters**.

Compensatory Responses
Acute blood loss triggers the following compensatory responses:

1. Transcapillary refill: interstitial fluid moves into bloodstream (can add up to **1 liter** to plasma volume)
2. Sympathetic activation: tachycardia and peripheral vasoconstriction
3. ADH release: promotes renal water retention
4. Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system activation: vasoconstriction and sodium retention

Progressive Blood Loss

**Table 15.1**
*Classification System for Acute Blood Loss*

| Class | Blood Loss | Clinical Features |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | <15% (<750 mL) | Minimal symptoms; transcapillary refill restores volume |
| **II** | 15–30% (750–1500 mL) | Compensated; urine output may decrease; splanchnic vasoconstriction |
| **III** | 31–40% (1500–2000 mL) | Decompensated shock; hypotension, oliguria, lactate rise |
| **IV** | >40% (>2000 mL) | Profound shock; multiorgan failure; may be irreversible |

Hemoglobin and Hematocrit
The use of the hemoglobin concentration and hematocrit (H & H) to evaluate the presence and
severity of acute blood loss is both common and inappropriate. Changes in hematocrit show a
poor correlation with blood volume deficits in acute hemorrhage (7). Acute blood loss involves the
loss of whole blood, which results in proportional decreases in plasma and erythrocytes. The H & H
will not decline immediately—it takes **8–12 hours** for transcapillary refill to dilute the blood.

### Resuscitation Basics

The Catheters
The determinants of flow through vascular catheters follow the Hagen-Poiseuille equation. Short,
large-bore peripheral vein catheters are much better suited for rapid infusions than longer central
venous catheters.

Rapid Infusion Catheters
Very large bore peripheral catheters are available in **7 French** and **9 French** sizes and can
deliver up to **1 liter per minute**.

Resuscitation Fluids

**Table 15.2**
*Resuscitation Fluids for Blood Loss*

| Type | Products | Principal Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Colloid** | 5% Albumin, 25% Albumin, HES, Dextrans | Expands plasma volume |
| **Crystalloid** | Isotonic Saline, Ringer's lactate, Normosol, Plasma-Lyte | Expands extracellular volume |
| **RBC Concentrate** | Packed RBCs | Increases O2 content |
| **Plasma** | Fresh Frozen Plasma, Liquid Plasma | Provides procoagulant proteins |
| **Plasma Precipitate** | Cryoprecipitate, Fibrinogen Concentrate | Increases fibrinogen levels |
| **Platelets** | Multiple-Donor Platelets, Apheresis Platelets | Increases circulating platelets |
| **Whole Blood** | Type-Specific Blood, Group O Blood | All of the above |

Crystalloids and Blood Loss
The current consensus is that fatal outcomes in hemorrhagic shock result from persistent bleeding and
coagulopathy, and that aggressive crystalloid use can aggravate ongoing blood loss by promoting
dilutional coagulopathy.

Rapid Infusion Systems
Pressurized infusion systems can deliver warmed blood products at rates up to **1 liter per minute**,
using pressures up to **300 mm Hg**.

**Table 15.3**
*Infusion of Packed RBCs*

| Method | 18-G Catheter | 16-G Catheter | 7-Fr RIC |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Unit PRBCs – No Dilution | 5 mL/min | 7 mL/min | 49 mL/min |
| 1 Unit + 100 mL Saline | 39 mL/min | 70 mL/min | 448 mL/min |
| Pressurized (300 mm Hg) | — | 183 mL/min | 1000 mL/min |

### Resuscitation Strategies

**Table 15.4**
*Asanguinous Resuscitation Volumes for Class II Hemorrhage*

| Step | Formula |
|---|---|
| 1. Estimate normal plasma volume (PV) | PV = 40 mL/kg (males) or 36 mL/kg (females) |
| 2. Estimate % loss | Class II: 15–30% Loss |
| 3. Calculate plasma volume deficit (PVD) | PVD = PV × % Loss |
| 4. Estimate resuscitation volume (RV) | RV = PVD × 1 (colloids) or PVD × 3 (crystalloids) |

Permissive Hypotension
Aggressive fluid resuscitation can exacerbate bleeding before hemorrhage is controlled. Reducing
fluid resuscitation allows blood pressures to remain lower than normal (systolic BP **80–90 mm Hg** or
mean BP **50 mm Hg**) until bleeding is controlled. Monitor plasma lactate levels and urine output.

Coagulopathy
One of the principal fears is persistent blood loss from coagulopathy. The major culprit in
trauma-induced coagulopathy (TIC) is activation of protein C from endothelial injury. Studies of
combat casualties showed TIC was present on admission in **40%** of cases, and created a **6-fold**
increase in mortality rate (31).

Hemostatic Resuscitation
The 1:1:1 replacement strategy: 1 unit FFP for every 1 unit packed RBCs, and 1 platelet pack for
every 5 units of PRBCs and FFP.

Adjunctive Measures:
- Calcium supplementation to maintain ionized calcium **1.2–1.3 mmol/L**
- Cryoprecipitate (2 units) for fibrinogen <**100 mg/dL**
- Tranexamic acid: **1 g IV** bolus, then **1 g over 8 hours**

End-Points:
- Fibrinogen >**100 mg/dL**
- INR <**1.5**
- aPTT <**1.5 times normal**
- Platelet count ≥**100,000/μL**

Thromboelastography
Thromboelastography (TEG) provides information on clot initiation, progression, strength, and
lysis from whole blood samples.

**Table 15.5**
*Interventions Based on Thromboelastography*

| TEG Parameter | Abnormal Value | Intervention |
|---|---|---|
| R (Reaction time) | >9 min | Transfuse plasma, or reverse anticoagulants |
| k (Kinetic time) | >2.5 min | Transfuse plasma |
| α angle | <65° | Cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate |
| MA (max amplitude) | <55 mm | Transfuse platelets |
| LY30 (lysis in 30 min) | >3% | Tranexamic acid |

### Postresuscitation Injury

An apparently successful resuscitation can be followed in **48–72 hours** by progressive multiorgan
failure. The mortality rate averages **50–60%** (37).

Pathophysiology
Postresuscitation injury is a form of reperfusion injury that originates in the splanchnic circulation,
where reperfusion of ischemic bowel releases proinflammatory cytokines.

Management
No specific therapy exists. Preventive measures include rapid reversal of shock, limiting crystalloid
fluids and RBC products.

### A Final Word

The resuscitation of hemorrhagic shock should incorporate:

1. Use short, large-bore catheters; avoid hypothermia (warm infusates to 37° C)
2. Restrict crystalloid fluids to **1–2 liters** before using blood products
3. Replace blood components in a 1:1:1 ratio
4. Consider permissive hypotension (systolic **80–90 mm Hg** or mean **50 mm Hg**)
5. Use thromboelastography (or ROTEM) to guide hemostatic management
