Chapter 46: Disorders of Movement

“When we contemplate this life, we see motion as its principal characteristic, and when we take a farther view, we see that this motion must necessarily waste the machine in which it resides.” — John Young

This chapter describes three movement disorders encountered in the ICU:

  1. Involuntary movements (seizures)
  2. Weak or ineffective movements (neuromuscular weakness)
  3. No movements (drug-induced paralysis)

Introduction

導讀摘要

  • 癲癇重積狀態（Status Epilepticus）為內科急症，第一線治療為 lorazepam（0.1 mg/kg IV）或 diazepam；第二線包括 fosphenytoin（20 mg PE/kg）、valproic acid、levetiracetam；頑固型需持續輸注 midazolam、propofol 或 pentobarbital。
  • 非痙攣性癲癇重積（NCSE）臨床表現在ICU多樣化（意識混亂、昏迷、myoclonus等），確診困難，需EEG持續監測。
  • ICU神經肌肉病變：**Critical Illness Myopathy（CIM）**特徵為對稱、遠端>近端無力、感覺正常；Critical Illness Polyneuropathy（CIP）為遠端軸索性神經病變；兩者皆與敗血症、皮質類固醇相關，多為可逆性。
  • Guillain-Barré Syndrome（GBS）：上行性無力、反射消失、可進展至呼吸衰竭；CSF呈白蛋白細胞分離（蛋白質升高但細胞數正常）；需與重症肌無力（Myasthenia Gravis）鑑別，後者有疲乏性且CSF正常。
  • 神經肌肉阻斷劑（NMBAs）：succinylcholine（去極化，1 mg/kg，最快 onset但致高血鉀）；rocuronium（非去極化，0.6–1 mg/kg）；cisatracurium（器官非依賴性代謝）；逆轉可用 neostigmine 或 sugammadex（專門針對rocuronium/vecuronium）。

Seizures

The risk of seizures in critically ill patients is determined primarily by the presence and type of brain injury rather than the general condition of the patient (e.g., severity of shock).

Conditions Producing New-Onset Seizures

The most common conditions that produce new-onset seizures in ICU patients include:

  • Stroke (ischemic or hemorrhagic)
  • Traumatic brain injury
  • Intracranial hemorrhage
  • Metabolic disturbances (hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, uremia, hepatic encephalopathy)
  • Drug toxicity or withdrawal (alcohol, benzodiazepines, opioids)
  • Infection (meningitis, encephalitis, sepsis)
  • Eclampsia
  • Hypoxia-ischemia
  • Brain tumor

Status Epilepticus

Status epilepticus is a condition in which seizures either persist or recur before the patient recovers from the prior one. It is the principal focus of this section and constitutes a medical emergency.

Clinical Manifestations of Nonconvulsive Status Epilepticus

Nonconvulsive status epilepticus (NCSE) is characterized by altered mental status without obvious motor manifestations and can be difficult to diagnose.

TABLE 46.1 — Clinical Manifestations of Nonconvulsive Status Epilepticus

Treatment of Status Epilepticus

TABLE 46.2 — Drug Regimens for Status Epilepticus

Line Drug Dosing Notes
1st line Lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg IV; may repeat once Preferred benzodiazepine for established SE
Diazepam 0.15–0.2 mg/kg IV; may repeat Alternative benzodiazepine
Midazolam 0.2 mg/kg IM or intranasal If IV access unavailable
2nd line Fosphenytoin 20 mg PE/kg IV Monitor for hypotension
Valproic acid 20–40 mg/kg IV Alternative; monitor liver function
Levetiracetam 20–60 mg/kg IV Alternative; favorable side effect profile
Phenobarbital 15–20 mg/kg IV Alternative

Refractory Status Epilepticus

When seizures persist despite adequate doses of first- and second-line agents, the condition is classified as refractory status epilepticus and requires continuous infusion therapy.

TABLE 46.3 — Drug Regimens for Refractory Status Epilepticus

Drug Loading Dose Maintenance Infusion
Midazolam 0.2 mg/kg IV bolus 0.05–2 mg/kg/hr
Propofol 1–2 mg/kg IV bolus 20–200 mcg/kg/min
Pentobarbital 5–15 mg/kg IV bolus 0.5–5 mg/kg/hr
Valproic acid 20–40 mg/kg IV 1–4 mg/kg/hr

EEG should be monitored continuously; goal is suppression of seizure activity.

Neuromuscular Weakness

Neuromuscular weakness in the ICU can result from disorders of the nerves, muscles, or neuromuscular junction.

Critical Illness Myopathy

Critical illness myopathy (CIM) is an increasingly recognized cause of weakness in ICU patients. Risk factors include:

  • Sepsis
  • Systemic inflammation
  • Corticosteroid use
  • Neuromuscular blocking agents
  • Prolonged immobility

Clinical features:

  • Diffuse, symmetric, distal > proximal weakness
  • Decreased reflexes
  • Sensory examination typically normal
  • Reduced compound muscle action potential (CMAP) on electrophysiologic testing
  • Often reversible if underlying condition improves

Critical Illness Polyneuropathy

Critical illness polyneuropathy (CIP) is a distal axonopathy that affects peripheral nerves. It is associated with sepsis and systemic inflammation.

Clinical features:

  • Diffuse, symmetric weakness
  • Decreased reflexes (may be preserved early)
  • Sensory deficits in a stocking-glove distribution
  • Often reversible in survivors of sepsis

Guillain-Barré Syndrome

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is an acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy often preceded by infection.

Clinical features:

  • Ascending weakness beginning in the legs
  • Areflexia
  • Sensory symptoms (paresthesias)
  • Can progress to respiratory failure
  • Autonomic dysfunction (cardiac arrhythmias, blood pressure instability)

TABLE 46.4 — Features That Distinguish Guillain-Barré Syndrome from Myasthenia Gravis

Feature Guillain-Barré Syndrome Myasthenia Gravis
Reflexes Decreased or absent Normal (or absent in severe disease)
Sensory involvement Often present Absent
Fatigability No Yes
Progression Over days to weeks Fluctuating
CSF Albuminocytologic dissociation Normal

Myasthenia Gravis

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disorder affecting the neuromuscular junction (antibodies against acetylcholine receptors or MuSK proteins).

Clinical features:

  • Fatigable weakness (worsens with activity)
  • Ocular symptoms (ptosis, diplopia) — often the initial presentation
  • Bulbar symptoms (dysphagia, dysarthria)
  • Proximal muscle weakness
  • Respiratory muscle involvement (myasthenic crisis)

Drugs that can exacerbate myasthenia gravis:

TABLE 46.5 — Drugs That Can Exacerbate Myasthenia Gravis

Category Examples
Antibiotics Aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, macrolides, penicillins
Cardiovascular drugs Beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, class Ia antiarrhythmics (procainamide, quinidine, disopyramide)
Neuromuscular blockers Both depolarizing and non-depolarizing agents
Others High-dose steroids, inhalational anesthetics, magnesium, lithium

Neuromuscular Blocking Agents

Neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs) are used in the ICU to facilitate mechanical ventilation and to reduce oxygen consumption in conditions such as ARDS.

TABLE 46.6 — Properties of Commonly-Used Neuromuscular Blocking Agents

Drug Class Intubating Dose (IV) Onset Clinical Duration Notes
Succinylcholine Depolarizing 1 mg/kg 30–60 sec 6–10 min Fastest onset; causes hyperkalemia
Rocuronium Non-depolarizing 0.6–1 mg/kg 60–90 sec 30–45 min Intermediate duration
Vecuronium Non-depolarizing 0.1 mg/kg 90–120 sec 45–60 min Hepatic/renal excretion
Cisatracurium Non-depolarizing 0.1–0.2 mg/kg 90–120 sec 45–60 min Organ-independent metabolism

Monitoring: Train-of-four (TOF) stimulation should be used to monitor depth of blockade. Target TOF ratio depends on clinical goal (e.g., shallow blockade for ventilation vs. deep paralysis for ARDS).

Reversal agents:

  • Neostigmine (for non-depolarizing agents) — 0.5–2 mg IV with glycopyrrolate or atropine
  • Sugammadex (for rocuronium or vecuronium) — 2–4 mg/kg for routine reversal; 16 mg/kg for deep blockade

Drug-Induced Paralysis

When ICU patients require chemical paralysis to reduce work of breathing, facilitate mechanical ventilation, or prevent agitation that interferes with ventilation, depolarizing or non-depolarizing neuromuscular blocking agents are employed. The pharmacology and complications are addressed above.

Important considerations:

  • Paralysis should be accompanied by adequate sedation and analgesia
  • Continuous infusion is preferred for prolonged paralysis
  • Monitor depth of blockade with train-of-four
  • Daily assessment of need for continued paralysis
  • Prolonged paralysis can lead to critical illness myopathy or polyneuropathy
